A SaaS launch video rarely gets stuck because nobody can render one. It gets stuck because the first cut arrives with too many messages, nobody knows what to approve, and the moment somebody asks for a change the team is back at zero.

The useful approval question is not **does this look polished?** It is: **does this film make one credible promise to the right viewer, and can we still change it after the meeting?**

This checklist is how I would get a short launch trailer through a small team without turning approval into a week of vague comments. It works whether you are briefing an agency or using an agent-first tool such as [VideoFlow Studio](https://studio.videoflow.dev/), which turns a product URL into an editable motion-graphics film from your terminal.

## 1. Approve the job before you approve the shots

Start with one sentence: *After watching this, what should this specific viewer understand or do?*

For a waitlist launch, that might be “request access because this replaces a painful manual handoff.” For a release, it might be “try the new workflow because the old workaround is gone.” Do not ask a 30-second trailer to introduce the company, list every feature, explain the pricing, and close the sale. That is a homepage in disguise.

Write down the audience, one promise, and one next action. If the team cannot agree on those three things, pausing is cheaper than polishing the wrong animation. This is the same discipline behind [turning customer research into a SaaS launch video brief](https://tools-and-how-tos.github.io/2026/09/22/how-to-turn-customer-research-into-a-saas-launch-video-brief/): evidence first, scenes second.

## 2. Choose one narrative spine

A good product trailer is not a sequence of feature cards. It carries one idea from the opening tension to the final action. Pick one of these shapes:

- **Pain to relief:** show the annoying task, then the simpler path.
- **Before to after:** make the contrast visible, not merely claimed.
- **One decisive capability:** follow a single feature that changes the buyer's day.
- **Release moment:** show what is newly possible and why it matters now.

Put the spine at the top of the review doc. Then label every proposed scene “supports it,” “proves it,” or “remove.” If a scene belongs to none of those, save it for another asset. A focused first cut is also much easier to turn into variants later.

## 3. Make the first cut reviewable, not final

The first cut should give reviewers something concrete to react to: the opening hook, the order of ideas, the product proof, and the closing ask. It does not need to settle every transition.

## 4. Review the rendered frames, not just the plan

A storyboard can hide the problem that matters most: whether the actual rendered film can be read at speed. Review the export on the device and crop where it will be seen. Then check:

- Can a viewer understand the opening in the first few seconds?
- Is the headline readable against the background?
- Does the product footage prove the claim, or merely decorate it?
- Do logo, type, and colors stay consistent from scene to scene?
- Is the end card visible long enough to act on?

Ask reviewers to leave comments in a constrained form: timestamp, issue, desired outcome. “Make it punchier” is not a note; “at 00:12, the product proof arrives after the claim—swap them” is a note someone can execute.

## 5. Separate message approval from finish approval

Do two short passes instead of one giant review meeting.

**Message pass:** Is the viewer, promise, proof, and next action correct? If not, change the structure.

**Finish pass:** Are the timing, contrast, alignment, copy, and audio ready? If not, change the craft without reopening the story.

This separation prevents a misplaced pixel from crowding out a missing customer benefit. It also makes stakeholder feedback easier to reconcile: product owns accuracy, marketing owns the promise, and the maker owns execution.

## 6. Keep the version editable after approval

Approval is not immunity from change. A founder may need a new end card, sales may want a vertical cut, or a launch date may move. Preserve the plan, assets, and editable source—not just an MP4 with “final-final” in the filename.

## The five-minute approval call

Before you release the final render, ask everyone to answer only these questions:

1. Who is this for?
2. What one promise does it make?
3. What proves that promise on screen?
4. What should the viewer do next?
5. Can we change the source if the answer changes tomorrow?

If those answers are crisp, you have a launch asset rather than a handsome distraction. If they are not, fix the brief or the cut before spending another hour on motion.
